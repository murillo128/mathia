# PC-194 — subunit reciprocal powers preserve the Fourier classifier, but the quadratic power creates prime-shell zeros

**Status:** `EXACT-DERIVED` + `LITERATURE+DERIVED` + `DECISIVE-NEGATIVE` for the exponent deformation of the minimally renormalized reciprocal cyclotomic amplitude.

For `n>1` and `alpha>0`, put

\[
G_{n,\alpha}(x)=\Phi_n(e^{-|x|})^{-\alpha}-1.
\tag{1}
\]

PC-192 proves that every non-prime-power shell has a nonzero real Fourier zero for every `alpha>0`, while PC-193 proves that at `alpha=1` the prime-power shells have strictly negative Fourier transform at every real frequency. Two exact facts sharpen the open exponent direction.

First, the full prime-power sign classification persists for the entire subunit range:

\[
\boxed{
0<\alpha\le 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n=p^k
\iff
\widehat G_{n,\alpha}(t)<0\ \text{for every }t\in\mathbb R.
}
\tag{2}
\]

Second, this zero-free prime-power behavior is not stable under the most elementary higher reciprocal power. At `alpha=2`, for every sufficiently large prime `p`, the prime shell itself has at least two nonzero positive Fourier zeros. More precisely, if

\[
t_p=2p^{3/4},
\tag{3}
\]

then

\[
\boxed{
\lim_{p\to\infty}p^3\,\widehat{(-G_{p,2})}(t_p)
=
\frac34-4\bigl(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)\bigr)<0.
}
\tag{4}
\]

Since `-G_{p,2}` has positive Fourier transform at frequency zero and again has a positive `t^{-2}` tail at fixed `p`, (4) forces two sign crossings for all sufficiently large primes.

Thus the scalar real-Fourier zero-free criterion of PC-193 is robust under fractional weakening `0<alpha<=1`, but it already fails at the canonical quadratic reciprocal amplitude. The new zeros are generated by a mesoscopic endpoint/refinement boundary layer, not by zeta data. This is a decisive negative for treating the zero-free/zero-bearing status of a freely chosen reciprocal power as an RH-strength Prime-Circle spectral invariant.

## 1. Prime powers reduce to prime shells for every exponent

Let `n=p^k` and `a=p^{k-1}`. The standard identity

\[
\Phi_{p^k}(z)=\Phi_p(z^a)
\tag{5}
\]

gives

\[
G_{p^k,\alpha}(x)=G_{p,\alpha}(a x),
\qquad
\widehat G_{p^k,\alpha}(t)
=\frac1a\widehat G_{p,\alpha}\!\left(\frac{t}{a}\right).
\tag{6}
\]

It therefore suffices to analyze a prime level. For a prime `p`, define

\[
R_p(x):=\Phi_p(e^{-|x|})^{-1}
=\frac{1-e^{-|x|}}{1-e^{-p|x|}},
\qquad
H_p(x):=1-R_p(x)=-G_{p,1}(x).
\tag{7}
\]

PC-193 proves the strict Fourier positivity

\[
\widehat H_p(t)>0
\qquad(t\in\mathbb R),
\tag{8}
\]

using the exact factorization into the exponential kernel and the strip-Poisson hyperbolic-sine ratio. Also

\[
0\le H_p(x)\le H_p(0)=1-\frac1p<1,
\qquad H_p\in L^1(\mathbb R).
\tag{9}
\]

These two elementary bounds are enough to propagate the sign through all subunit reciprocal powers.

## 2. Every `0<alpha<=1` is an absolutely positive power-series deformation of PC-193

For `0<alpha<1`, set

\[
F_\alpha(u):=1-(1-u)^\alpha.
\tag{10}
\]

Its Taylor series on `|u|<1` is

\[
F_\alpha(u)
=
\sum_{m\ge1}c_m(\alpha)u^m,
\qquad
c_m(\alpha)=(-1)^{m+1}\binom{\alpha}{m}>0.
\tag{11}
\]

At `alpha=1`, only `c_1=1` remains. Since `R_p=1-H_p`,

\[
-H_{p,\alpha}^{\rm sign}:=G_{p,\alpha}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
H_{p,\alpha}:=-G_{p,\alpha}
=1-R_p^\alpha
=F_\alpha(H_p).
\tag{12}
\]

Using (9), the series converges not only pointwise but in `L^1`. Indeed, with `a_p=1-1/p`,

\[
\|H_p^m\|_1
\le a_p^{m-1}\|H_p\|_1,
\tag{13}
\]

and

\[
\sum_{m\ge1}c_m(\alpha)a_p^{m-1}<\infty.
\tag{14}
\]

Each pointwise power `H_p^m` is positive definite: equivalently, its Fourier transform is the nonnegative convolution power

\[
\widehat{H_p^m}
=(2\pi)^{1-m}
\underbrace{\widehat H_p*\cdots*\widehat H_p}_{m\text{ factors}}
\ge0.
\tag{15}
\]

Termwise Fourier transformation is therefore justified by the `L^1` convergence, and the first term is already strictly positive. Hence

\[
\widehat H_{p,\alpha}(t)
=
\sum_{m\ge1}c_m(\alpha)\widehat{H_p^m}(t)
\ge
\alpha\widehat H_p(t)>0.
\tag{16}
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\widehat G_{p,\alpha}(t)<0
\qquad(0<\alpha\le1,\ t\in\mathbb R).
}
\tag{17}
\]

Equation (6) propagates (17) to every prime power. PC-192 supplies the converse: for every non-prime-power and every `alpha>0`, the transform changes sign and has a nonzero real zero. Combining the two gives (2).

The positivity mechanism in this section is classical in form. Nonnegative power series preserve positive-definite kernels by the Schur product theorem; the dimension-free classification of such entrywise positivity preservers is the Schoenberg--Rudin absolutely-monotone theory. In particular, the coefficient boundary at `alpha=1` is not an artifact of this proof: for `alpha>1`, the quadratic coefficient

\[
c_2(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}2
\tag{18}
\]

is negative, so `F_alpha` leaves the universal absolutely-monotone positivity-preserver class. This does not by itself prove failure for the special cyclotomic kernel, which is why the quadratic shell is tested directly next.

## 3. The quadratic prime-shell amplitude has an exact outer limit plus a negative boundary layer

Set `alpha=2` and write

\[
H_{p,2}(x):=-G_{p,2}(x)=1-R_p(x)^2.
\tag{19}
\]

Separate the `p->infinity` outer profile

\[
h(x):=1-(1-e^{-|x|})^2
=2e^{-|x|}-e^{-2|x|}.
\tag{20}
\]

Then the difference is exactly

\[
D_p(x):=H_{p,2}(x)-h(x)
=(1-e^{-|x|})^2
\left[1-(1-e^{-p|x|})^{-2}\right]
\le0.
\tag{21}
\]

The outer transform is elementary:

\[
\boxed{
\widehat h(t)
=
\frac{12}{(1+t^2)(4+t^2)}>0.
}
\tag{22}
\]

So any loss of Fourier positivity must come from the refinement-scale boundary layer `|x|=O(1/p)` hidden in (21).

## 4. The mesoscopic scale `t~p^(3/4)` resolves that boundary layer

Take

\[
t_p=c\,p^{3/4},
\qquad c>0.
\tag{23}
\]

From (22),

\[
\lim_{p\to\infty}p^3\widehat h(t_p)
=\frac{12}{c^4}.
\tag{24}
\]

For the boundary term, use evenness and the change of variables `u=px`:

\[
\begin{aligned}
p^3\widehat D_p(t_p)
&=
2\int_0^\infty
\bigl[p(1-e^{-u/p})\bigr]^2
\left[1-(1-e^{-u})^{-2}\right]
\cos(c p^{-1/4}u)\,du.
\end{aligned}
\tag{25}
\]

For each fixed `u`, the first bracket tends to `u^2` and the cosine tends to `1`. Moreover

\[
0\le p(1-e^{-u/p})\le u,
\tag{26}
\]

while

\[
u^2\left[(1-e^{-u})^{-2}-1\right]
\tag{27}
\]

is integrable on `(0,infinity)`: it is bounded at zero and decays exponentially at infinity. Dominated convergence therefore gives

\[
\lim_{p\to\infty}p^3\widehat D_p(t_p)
=
2\int_0^\infty
u^2\left[1-(1-e^{-u})^{-2}\right]du.
\tag{28}
\]

The integral is exact. Since

\[
(1-e^{-u})^{-2}
=
\sum_{m\ge0}(m+1)e^{-mu},
\tag{29}
\]

Tonelli on the nonnegative opposite sign gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
2\int_0^\infty
u^2\left[1-(1-e^{-u})^{-2}\right]du
&=-2\sum_{m\ge1}(m+1)\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-mu}du\\
&=-4\sum_{m\ge1}\left(\frac1{m^2}+\frac1{m^3}\right)\\
&=\boxed{-4\bigl(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)\bigr)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{30}
\]

Combining (24) and (30),

\[
\boxed{
\lim_{p\to\infty}p^3\widehat H_{p,2}(c p^{3/4})
=
\frac{12}{c^4}-4\bigl(\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)\bigr).
}
\tag{31}
\]

Choosing `c=2` gives (4). The negativity is separated from zero without numerical input: `zeta(2)>1` and `zeta(3)>1`, so the right side is smaller than `3/4-8`.

The exponent `3/4` is not fitted. It is forced by balancing the `t^{-4}` outer Fourier tail in (22) against the `p^{-3}` total mass of the exact refinement boundary layer (21).

## 5. Large prime shells therefore acquire at least two real Fourier zeros at `alpha=2`

At zero frequency,

\[
\widehat H_{p,2}(0)
=
\int_{\mathbb R}H_{p,2}(x)\,dx>0,
\tag{32}
\]

because `0<R_p(x)<1` away from radial infinity. Equation (31) with `c=2` says that for every sufficiently large prime `p`,

\[
\widehat H_{p,2}(2p^{3/4})<0.
\tag{33}
\]

There is also an exact positive high-frequency tail at each fixed prime. The endpoint expansion of (7) is

\[
R_p(0)=\frac1p,
\qquad
R_p'(0+)=\frac{p-1}{2p},
\tag{34}
\]

so

\[
H_{p,2}'(0+)
=-\frac{p-1}{p^2}.
\tag{35}
\]

Two integrations by parts, exactly as in PC-192, give

\[
\boxed{
\widehat H_{p,2}(t)
=
\frac{2(p-1)}{p^2t^2}+o(t^{-2})>0
\quad(|t|\to\infty).
}
\tag{36}
\]

Continuity, (32), (33), and (36) force at least two positive-frequency zeros of `widehat H_{p,2}`, hence the same two zeros of `widehat G_{p,2}`, for all sufficiently large primes.

This is a matched arithmetic failure, not merely a composite control: the shell itself is prime, all non-anchor vertices are primitive, and only the reciprocal exponent has changed from `1` to `2`.

## 6. Prior-art and novelty audit

The general positivity-preserver mechanism in Section 2 is classical. The Schur product theorem makes nonnegative power series preserve positive-definite kernels; the corresponding dimension-free characterization by absolutely monotone functions is classical Schoenberg--Rudin theory (see W. Rudin, *Positive definite sequences and absolutely monotonic functions*, Duke Math. J. 26 (1959), 617--622). Modern positivity-preserver literature states the same boundary explicitly. PC-193 already supplies the only special positive-definite kernel needed here through the strip-Poisson transform anchored in `SOURCES.md`.

Directed searches for Fourier transforms of reciprocal cyclotomic amplitudes, noninteger powers of `Phi_p(e^{-x})`, the profile `1-[(1-e^{-x})/(1-e^{-px})]^alpha`, and exponent-dependent cyclotomic Fourier-zero criteria did not locate the Prime-Circle statements (2) or (31). That absence is **not** treated as a novelty claim. The subunit theorem is a direct application of classical positivity closure to PC-193, while the quadratic failure is an exact boundary-layer consequence of the elementary prime cyclotomic formula.

The zeta values in (30) are also not Riemann-zero information. They are the ordinary positive constants produced by the geometric-series moment

\[
\sum_{m\ge1}(m+1)m^{-3}=\zeta(2)+\zeta(3).
\tag{37}
\]

No complex spectral parameter, functional equation, gamma completion, or critical-line mechanism enters.

## 7. What is closed and what remains open

PC-193 left the exponent `alpha!=1` prime-power sector open. The present result divides that direction into a rigid positive range and an explicit failure point:

\[
0<\alpha\le1:
\quad\text{the exact prime-power Fourier-sign classifier survives};
\tag{38}
\]

\[
\alpha=2:
\quad\text{large prime shells themselves acquire real Fourier zeros}.
\tag{39}
\]

The interval `1<alpha<2` is not classified here, nor are the exact first failure prime, zero locations, multiplicities, or possible isolated exponents above two. Those are secondary scalar questions unless a geometry-forced exponent is identified. The important research boundary is already decisive: **real Fourier zero existence in this one-shell reciprocal family is deformation-dependent even on prime shells**.

Therefore a surviving Prime-Circle spectral route should not promote a convenient reciprocal exponent and then interpret its real zeros as intrinsic arithmetic spectrum. It needs a source-forced exponent/operator, a quantitative relation among spectral locations stable under natural deformations, or genuinely cross-shell/nonlocal information before scalarization. The matrix-valued, cross-level, signed-shell, singular-boundary, and global uniformization/monodromy directions remain outside this result.

## Research consequence

The exponent-one sign theorem of PC-193 is not an isolated accident: classical positive-definite closure extends it throughout `0<alpha<=1`. But that same scalar family already manufactures Fourier zeros on genuine prime shells at `alpha=2`, through an exact mesoscopic boundary-layer competition. This turns the open exponent direction into a useful falsification boundary rather than a candidate RH signal. Any future use of reciprocal-amplitude Fourier zeros must first justify why its exponent or operator is forced by Prime-Circle geometry and why the claimed spectral relation survives this deformation test.
