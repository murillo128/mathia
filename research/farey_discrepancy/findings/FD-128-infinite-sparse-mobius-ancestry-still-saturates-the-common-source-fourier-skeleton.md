# FD-128 — infinite sparse Möbius ancestry still saturates the common-source Fourier skeleton

**Status:** `EXACT-DERIVED + COMMON-SOURCE-CONSTRUCTION + FLOOR-QUOTIENT-FOURIER-SKELETON + EXACT-SQUAREFREE-UNIT-INCREMENTS + INFINITE-SPARSE-PRIME-MOBIUS-ANCESTRY + SQUARE-ROOT-ENVELOPE + SUPEREXPONENTIAL-SPARSE-SATURATION + NEGATIVE/ROUTE-RESTRICTION`.

`FD-127` shows that any **fixed finite** packet of exact Möbius prime-direction identities can be absorbed by one squarefree-unit common source while the `FD-118` Fourier skeleton still approaches its equality ray on repeated-square horizons. The obvious next possibility is that the obstruction disappears as soon as the number of genuine prime directions grows without bound.

It does not, at least on sufficiently sparse horizons. The arithmetic rank may grow to infinity while the same source retains the critical square-root envelope and asymptotically saturates the Fourier skeleton. What matters is therefore not cardinality of the enforced prime family by itself, but its quantitative breadth at the active scale.

Fix any finite set of primes `P_0`. Then there exist an infinite set of primes

\[
P_0\subset S,
\tag{1}
\]

one infinite increment sequence `a=(a_n)`, and horizons `H_j\to\infty` such that

\[
a_1=1,\qquad
a_n=0\quad(n\text{ nonsquarefree}),\qquad
a_n\in\{-1,+1\}\quad(n\text{ squarefree}),
\tag{2}
\]

and, for every `p in S`,

\[
\boxed{
a_{pn}=-a_n
\qquad(p\nmid n)
}
\tag{3}
\]

at every integer, not merely at retained horizons. Writing

\[
Y(x):=\sum_{n\le x}a_n,
\tag{4}
\]

the source can be chosen with the global critical envelope

\[
\boxed{|Y(x)|\ll_S\sqrt x.}
\tag{5}
\]

The prime set can be made genuinely sparse, for example with

\[
\#\{p\in S:p\le x\}=O_{P_0}(\sqrt{\log\log x}),
\tag{6}
\]

while still being infinite.

For the `FD-118` skeleton

\[
\mathcal Q_H
:=
\left\{\left\lfloor\frac Hm\right\rfloor:1\le m\le H\right\},
\qquad
\tau_H(k):=\left\lfloor\frac Hk\right\rfloor,
\tag{7}
\]

\[
A_H(k)
:=
\sum_{d\mid k}d\,Y(\tau_H(d)),
\qquad
\mathcal E_H^{\mathcal Q}(Y)
:=
\sum_{k\in\mathcal Q_H}\frac{|A_H(k)|^2}{k^2},
\tag{8}
\]

the horizons may be chosen so sparse that

\[
\frac1j\log\frac{H_j}{H_0}\longrightarrow\infty,
\tag{9}
\]

and there are integers `t_j` of the forced squarefree parity with

\[
Y(H_j)=t_j,
\qquad
|t_j-\sqrt{H_j}|\le1,
\tag{10}
\]

for which

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal E_{H_j}^{\mathcal Q}(Y)
=
t_j^2+o(H_j).
}
\tag{11}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\mathcal E_{H_j}^{\mathcal Q}(Y)}
{|Y(H_j)|^2}
\longrightarrow1.
}
\tag{12}
\]

Thus even **unbounded exact Möbius prime ancestry** does not by itself force a positive relative gap on the common-source Fourier skeleton. The surviving source question is quantitative: how much prime breadth must be visible at scale `H`, or how dense must the multiplicative compatibility be, before the free core and its triangular inverse lose the capacity to track the equality ray?

## 1. Choose an infinite ancestry set with summable semigroup weights

The sparse-prime mechanism is the one already audited in `FD-028`, now transplanted to the Fourier equality ray. Let

\[
c_0
:=
\frac1{\zeta(2)}
\prod_{p\in P_0}\frac{p}{p+1}
>0.
\tag{13}
\]

Choose new primes `r_1<r_2<...`, outside `P_0`, so rapidly that

\[
r_j>\exp(\exp(j^2+10)),
\qquad
\sum_j\frac1{r_j}<\frac{c_0}{4},
\qquad
\sum_j\frac1{\sqrt{r_j}}<\infty.
\tag{14}
\]

Put `T={r_1,r_2,...}` and `S=P_0\cup T`. Let `D_S` be the squarefree multiplicative semigroup generated by `S`, and let `M_S` be the full multiplicative semigroup generated by `S`. Exactly as in `FD-028`,

\[
D_S(x)M_S(x)
=
\exp(o(\sqrt{\log x}))
=
x^{o(1)},
\tag{15}
\]

while

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{q\in D_S}q^{-1/2}
=
\prod_{p\in S}(1+p^{-1/2})
<\infty,
}
\tag{16}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{s\in M_S}s^{-1}
=
\prod_{p\in S}(1-p^{-1})^{-1}
<\infty.
}
\tag{17}
\]

The first product will transfer a square-root bound from a free core to the full source; the second controls the triangular inverse created by the exact prime-direction identities.

## 2. Factor every squarefree sign through the `S`-free core

Let

\[
\mathcal C_S
:=
\{m\ge1:m\text{ squarefree and }p\nmid m\text{ for every }p\in S\}.
\tag{18}
\]

Choose signs `b_m in {-1,+1}` on `\mathcal C_S`, with `b_1=1`, and write

\[
B(x)
:=
\sum_{\substack{m\le x\\m\in\mathcal C_S}}b_m,
\qquad
B(0):=0.
\tag{19}
\]

Every squarefree integer factors uniquely as

\[
n=qm,
\qquad
q\in D_S,\quad m\in\mathcal C_S.
\tag{20}
\]

Define

\[
a_n=
\begin{cases}
\mu(q)b_m,&n=qm\text{ as in (20)},\\
0,&n\text{ nonsquarefree}.
\end{cases}
\tag{21}
\]

Then (2)--(3) hold identically. Summing gives the exact source transform

\[
\boxed{
Y(x)
=
\sum_{\substack{q\in D_S\\q\le x}}
\mu(q)
B\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac xq\right\rfloor\right).
}
\tag{22}
\]

At a horizon `H`, put

\[
q_d:=\left\lfloor\frac Hd\right\rfloor.
\tag{23}
\]

Nested floors imply

\[
\left\lfloor\frac{q_d}{q}\right\rfloor=q_{qd},
\tag{24}
\]

with `q_k=0` for `k>H`, so

\[
\boxed{
Y(q_d)
=
\sum_{q\in D_S}\mu(q)B(q_{qd}).
}
\tag{25}
\]

This is the infinite-prime analogue of the finite triangular transform in `FD-127`.

The free core remains locally abundant. The counting argument of `FD-028` gives, on

\[
I_d:=(q_{d+1},q_d]\cap\mathbb Z,
\tag{26}
\]

\[
\#(\mathcal C_S\cap I_d)
\ge
\frac{3c_0}{4}|I_d|
-O_{P_0}(\sqrt{q_d})
-\pi_T(q_d).
\tag{27}
\]

For

\[
R:=\lfloor H^{1/4}\rfloor
\tag{28}
\]

one has uniformly for `1<=d<=R`

\[
|I_d|\asymp\frac H{d^2},
\qquad
\frac{\sqrt{q_d}}{|I_d|}
\ll
\frac{d^{3/2}}{\sqrt H}
\le H^{-1/8},
\tag{29}
\]

and the `\pi_T` term is negligible. Hence

\[
\boxed{
\#(\mathcal C_S\cap I_d)
\gg_{P_0}\frac H{d^2}
\qquad(1\le d\le R)
}
\tag{30}
\]

for sufficiently large `H`.

## 3. A sparse stage can hit the Fourier equality prefix exactly up to parity

Assume the core signs have already been fixed through an old horizon `H_*`, and let

\[
B_*:=\max_{n\le H_*}|B(n)|.
\tag{31}
\]

At stage `j`, set `\varepsilon_j:=1/(j+16)`, choose `H` arbitrarily large, and put

\[
R=\lfloor H^{1/4}\rfloor,
\qquad
Q=q_{R+1}.
\tag{32}
\]

Because (15) is subpolynomial and `B_*` is fixed while this stage chooses `H`, we may impose simultaneously

\[
Q>H_*,
\tag{33}
\]

\[
W_H:=(B_*+2)D_S(H)\le H^{\varepsilon_j/2},
\tag{34}
\]

\[
(1+W_H)M_S(R)\le H^{1/4},
\tag{35}
\]

and any desired additional lower bound on `H`, in particular one forcing (9).

First assign the fresh core signs on `(H_*,Q]` in balanced order. Then

\[
|B(x)|\le B_*+2
\qquad(x\le Q).
\tag{36}
\]

Choose the integer `t` of the forced squarefree parity nearest `sqrt(H)`. For `1<=d<=R`, choose integers `y_d` with the parity of the total number of squarefree integers at most `q_d` and

\[
y_1=t,
\qquad
\left|y_d-t\frac{\mu(d)}d\right|\le1
\quad(2\le d\le R).
\tag{37}
\]

For each `d<=R`, split the already frozen ancestry tail from (25):

\[
w_d
:=
\sum_{\substack{q\in D_S\\qd>R}}
\mu(q)B(q_{qd}).
\tag{38}
\]

Every argument in (38) is at most `Q`, so

\[
|w_d|\le W_H.
\tag{39}
\]

Define the desired core endpoints `z_d:=B(q_d)` recursively for `d=R,R-1,...,1` by

\[
\boxed{
z_d
=
y_d-w_d
-
\sum_{\substack{q\in D_S\\q>1,\ qd\le R}}
\mu(q)z_{qd}.
}
\tag{40}
\]

Every active index `qd` on the right is larger than `d`, so this is a finite triangular recurrence. Combining (38) and (40) gives

\[
\boxed{
Y(q_d)=y_d
\qquad(1\le d\le R).
}
\tag{41}
\]

Parity is exact, not approximate. Modulo `2`, the partition of every squarefree integer into its `S`-part and `S`-free core implies that (40) forces

\[
z_d
\equiv
\#\{m\le q_d:m\in\mathcal C_S\}
\pmod2.
\tag{42}
\]

Thus every endpoint in (40) has the parity of an actual assignment of `+-1` core signs.

## 4. The semigroup inverse remains smaller than the core capacity

On the finite index range `1<=d<=R`, write `T_pz(d)=z_{pd}` when `pd<=R` and zero otherwise. The active forward operator in (40) is

\[
\prod_{p\in S}(I-T_p),
\tag{43}
\]

and its finite inverse is the truncation of

\[
\prod_{p\in S}(I-T_p)^{-1}
=
\sum_{s\in M_S}T_s.
\tag{44}
\]

Using (17), (37), and (39),

\[
|z_d|
\le
C_S\frac{\sqrt H}{d}
+
(1+W_H)M_S(R),
\tag{45}
\]

for a finite constant `C_S`. By (35),

\[
\boxed{
|z_d|
\ll_S
\frac{\sqrt H}{d}+H^{1/4}.
}
\tag{46}
\]

Therefore

\[
|z_d-z_{d+1}|
\ll_S
\frac{\sqrt H}{d}+H^{1/4}.
\tag{47}
\]

The same bound holds for the final jump to the frozen endpoint `B(Q)`, because (34) makes `B_*+2=o(H^{1/4})`. Comparing (47) with (30),

\[
\frac{\sqrt H/d}{H/d^2}
=
\frac d{\sqrt H}
\le H^{-1/4},
\tag{48}
\]

and

\[
\frac{H^{1/4}}{H/d^2}
=
\frac{d^2}{H^{3/4}}
\le H^{-1/4}.
\tag{49}
\]

Hence every prescribed endpoint jump is eventually strictly smaller than the number of available core signs in its interval, with exactly the required parity. Assign the signs interval by interval, ordering them with at most unit overshoot between endpoints. This extends the one permanent source without changing any earlier sign.

The construction also preserves a uniform square-root envelope. On `I_d`, (46) and bounded overshoot give

\[
|B(n)|
\ll_S
\frac{\sqrt H}{d}+H^{1/4}
\ll_S
\sqrt n,
\tag{50}
\]

because `n\asymp H/d` and `n\gg H^{3/4}` throughout the active prefix. On `(H_*,Q]`, balanced continuation preserves the previous maximum and hence the same square-root induction. Thus

\[
|B(n)|\ll_S\sqrt n
\tag{51}
\]

globally. Equation (22) and (16) then yield

\[
\boxed{
|Y(n)|
\le
\sum_{q\in D_S}
\left|B\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac nq\right\rfloor\right)\right|
\ll_S
\sqrt n\sum_{q\in D_S}q^{-1/2}
\ll_S\sqrt n.
}
\tag{52}
\]

This is stronger than the `1/2+epsilon` envelope needed in the older GCD construction `FD-028`.

## 5. The inherited lower prefix is cheap in `L^2`

The Fourier skeleton needs more than a pointwise envelope: the lower quotient prefix must have small total square mass. That is exactly why the balanced interval `(H_*,Q]` was installed before the top endpoints.

From (22) and (36), for every `x<=Q`,

\[
|Y(x)|
\le
(B_*+2)D_S(H)
=
W_H.
\tag{53}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
P_Y(Q)
:=
\sum_{n\le Q}|Y(n)|^2
\le
QW_H^2
\ll
H^{3/4+\varepsilon_j}.
}
\tag{54}
\]

This is the crucial distinction from the older GCD norm: the adaptive horizon can absorb the finite inherited source magnitude into a subpower factor while keeping the terminal amplitude at the true critical scale `sqrt(H)`.

## 6. Fourier energy approaches the equality ray

Fix a retained horizon `H=H_j`, write `t=t_j`, and define

\[
x_k:=Y(\tau_H(k)),
\qquad
x_k^{(0)}:=t\frac{\mu(k)}k,
\qquad
\delta_k:=x_k-x_k^{(0)}
\qquad(k\in\mathcal Q_H).
\tag{55}
\]

By (37) and (41),

\[
\delta_1=0,
\qquad
|\delta_k|\le1
\quad(2\le k\le R).
\tag{56}
\]

For `k>R`, one has `\tau_H(k)\le Q`. The floor-quotient involution is a bijection of `\mathcal Q_H`, so (54) gives

\[
\sum_{\substack{k\in\mathcal Q_H\\k>R}}
|Y(\tau_H(k))|^2
\le
P_Y(Q)
\ll
H^{3/4+\varepsilon_j}.
\tag{57}
\]

The equality-ray tail satisfies

\[
\sum_{k>R}
\left|t\frac{\mu(k)}k\right|^2
\ll
\frac{H}{R}
\ll H^{3/4}.
\tag{58}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\sum_{k\in\mathcal Q_H}|\delta_k|^2
\ll
H^{3/4+\varepsilon_j}.
}
\tag{59}
\]

Let

\[
\mathcal B(k):=\sum_{d\mid k}d\,\delta_d.
\tag{60}
\]

The elementary divisor-transform stability estimate proved in `FD-120` gives

\[
\sum_{k\in\mathcal Q_H}
\frac{|\mathcal B(k)|^2}{k^2}
\ll
(1+\log H)^2
\sum_{k\in\mathcal Q_H}|\delta_k|^2
\ll
H^{3/4+\varepsilon_j}(\log H)^2.
\tag{61}
\]

For the equality ray `x_k^(0)=t mu(k)/k`,

\[
\sum_{d\mid k}d\,x_d^{(0)}
=
t\sum_{d\mid k}\mu(d)
=
\begin{cases}
t,&k=1,\\
0,&k>1.
\end{cases}
\tag{62}
\]

Because `\delta_1=0`, the perturbation has zero first Fourier coordinate, so the cross term vanishes exactly. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal E_H^{\mathcal Q}(Y)
=
t^2
+
O\!\left(
H^{3/4+\varepsilon_j}(\log H)^2
\right).
}
\tag{63}
\]

Since `\varepsilon_j=1/(j+16)\to0`, the error in (63) is `o(H_j)`. Equations (10)--(12) follow.

Finally, because each stage may impose an arbitrary additional lower bound on its new horizon, require for example

\[
H_j\ge H_{j-1}^{\,j}.
\tag{64}
\]

Then

\[
\frac1j\log\frac{H_j}{H_0}\to\infty,
\tag{65}
\]

placing the construction explicitly inside the superexponential scheduling escape left open by `FD-124`.

## 7. Stress tests, prior art, and research consequence

The main falsification points were checked separately.

The factorization (20)--(25) was tested on finite toy prime sets by direct enumeration: the source transform and the descending recurrence (40) reproduce the prescribed quotient endpoints exactly. The Fourier equality ray was also checked independently: `sum_(d|k) mu(d)` kills every mode `k>1`, and the floor-quotient map is involutive on `\mathcal Q_H`. These computations are regression checks only; the proof is the exact algebra above.

The potentially dangerous estimates are all explicit. The infinite ancestry tail is frozen below `Q` before the active endpoints are chosen. The semigroup inverse uses arbitrary prime powers, hence `M_S` rather than `D_S`, and its reciprocal mass is finite by (17). Core capacity is not inferred from global density alone: (27)--(30) give the required interval lower bound uniformly through `R=H^(1/4)`. The final Fourier tail uses the lower-prefix `L^2` budget (54), not merely the global pointwise bound (52).

The sparse-prime decomposition itself is classical territory. Terence Tao, *A remark on partial sums involving the Möbius function*, Bull. Aust. Math. Soc. **81** (2010), 343--349, DOI `10.1017/S0004972709000884`, studies Möbius sums on semigroups generated by arbitrary prime sets. Marco Aymone, *A note on multiplicative functions resembling the Möbius function*, J. Number Theory **212** (2020), 113--121, DOI `10.1016/j.jnt.2019.10.025`, studies squarefree-supported multiplicative `+-1` functions with small partial sums. A fresh targeted audit also found recent Farey-discrepancy work, but no source asserting this Mathia-specific common-source Fourier-skeleton saturation with infinite prescribed prime ancestry. No global novelty claim is made for prime-set factorization, sparse semigroups, or multiplicative-function cancellation.

No external theorem is load-bearing here beyond literature boundaries already used in the line: the proof imports the internal sparse-semigroup counting/core-capacity mechanism of `FD-028` and the elementary Fourier operator bound of `FD-120`. Therefore `SOURCES.md` does not need a new dependency.

The frontier changes materially. `FD-127` ruled out **fixed finite** prime breadth. `FD-128` rules out cardinal growth by itself: even one infinite ancestry set, hence an unbounded number of exact prime directions below `H`, can remain compatible with critical common-source saturation if those directions are sparse enough and the retained horizons are allowed to be superexponential.

This does **not** settle the growing-prime problem on the repeated-square chain of `FD-126`--`FD-127`; the present construction uses adaptive horizons with much more slack. The surviving sharp question is therefore two-dimensional:

\[
\boxed{
\text{how fast must prime ancestry broaden relative to }H,
\text{ and how much horizon sparsity is needed to absorb it?}
}
\tag{66}
\]

A useful next theorem should price a quantitative scale parameter -- prime count, reciprocal prime mass, semigroup growth, core density loss, or triangular-inverse norm -- rather than the mere number of enforced relations. Any coercive source law must cross a scale-dependent threshold that the sparse infinite construction above deliberately stays below.
